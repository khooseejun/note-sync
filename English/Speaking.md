Speaking Script: Direct & Indirect Speech

(Part 1: Definition)

Hello everyone! Today, we're going to talk about a fundamental concept in English grammar: Direct and Indirect Speech, which is also known as Reported Speech.

Let's start with Direct Speech. Direct speech is when we quote the exact words spoken by someone. We always put these exact words inside quotation marks.

For example, if Lisa says, "I am learning English," and we report it directly, we write: "I am learning English," said Lisa. We can see her original words are preserved inside the quotes.

Now, what about Indirect Speech? Indirect speech is when we report what someone said, but we don't use their exact words. We are paraphrasing or reporting the meaning of their statement. Because of this, we often have to make changes to the tense, the pronouns, and words related to time.

So, if we take Lisa's statement and report it indirectly, it becomes: Lisa said that she was learning English.
Did you notice the changes?

· The quotation marks are gone.
· The pronoun "I" changed to "she".
· And the present continuous tense "am learning" became the past continuous "was learning".

So, to summarize: Direct Speech is like a direct quote, and Indirect Speech is like telling someone about what was said later on.

(Part 2: Special Cases
Now, let's move on to some special cases where the conversion rules are a bit different. These involve imperative sentences, questions, and exclamations.

First, Special Case 1: Imperative Sentences, or Commands and Requests.
When someone gives an order or makes a request,we don't use "that" in indirect speech. Instead, we use an infinitive verb, like "to close" or "to open." We also change the reporting verb to something like asked, told, ordered, or requested.

Let's take an example. The direct speech is: "Please close the door," she said.
In indirect speech,this becomes: She asked me to close the door.
We see the command"close the door" is transformed into "to close the door."

Next, Special Case 2: Questions.
Reporting questions is tricky because we have two types.

The first type is Yes/No questions. In indirect speech, we introduce them with "if" or "whether."
For example,the direct question: "Do you like coffee?" she asked.
Becomes:She asked if I liked coffee.
Notice how the question structure disappears,and it becomes a normal statement.

The second type is WH-questions—those starting with who, what, where, when, why, and how. When we report these, we do not use "if" or "whether." We keep the WH-word.
For example:"Where are you going?" he asked.
In indirect speech,it becomes: He asked where I was going.
Again,the word order changes from a question to a statement.

Finally, Special Case 3: Exclamatory Sentences.
These are sentences that express strong emotion,like surprise or joy. In indirect speech, we often need to explain the emotion rather than just convert it directly.
For example,the direct exclamation: "What a beautiful day!" she exclaimed.
We can report this as:She exclaimed that it was a beautiful day.
We use reporting verbs likeexclaimed, cried, or shouted to convey the strong feeling.

So, to wrap up, while the basic rules of tense and pronoun changes are crucial, paying attention to these special cases—commands, questions, and exclamations—is the key to mastering reported speech.
